# 1. Introduction

Deep learning has become an important research approach for automated skin-lesion image classification. However, the reliability of a classifier cannot be described by its predicted class alone. A model may produce an incorrect prediction with high confidence, while a low-confidence prediction may still be correct. For applications in which errors matter, an additional question is whether the model can identify predictions that are more likely to be incorrect and prioritize them for further review.

Confidence-based error detection commonly uses the predicted probability of the selected class as a measure of uncertainty. In this setting, lower confidence is treated as a signal of greater error risk. However, confidence and predictive correctness are not interchangeable. Guo et al. (2017) demonstrated that modern neural networks can be poorly calibrated, motivating careful evaluation of their probability estimates. The present study does not assess calibration directly; instead, it evaluates whether confidence-based scores can rank incorrect predictions above correct predictions.

A complementary signal is disagreement between models. Models with different architectures or learned representations may make different predictions for the same image. Such disagreement may indicate that a case is difficult for at least one model, although disagreement does not necessarily imply that either prediction is incorrect. Deep ensembles have been studied as a practical means of estimating predictive uncertainty (Lakshminarayanan et al., 2017), and disagreement has also been investigated as a source of complementary information in skin-lesion classification. For example, Dakhli and Barhoumi (2024) proposed an explainable ensemble approach based on Dempster–Shafer evidence fusion and model complementarity to improve classification performance on HAM10000. Their work establishes relevant precedent for studying disagreement in this domain, while addressing a different primary objective from the error-ranking question considered here.

This distinction matters because improving the final class prediction and identifying which predictions are likely to be wrong are related but different tasks. A fusion method may improve classification accuracy without necessarily producing the best ranking of a particular model's errors. Conversely, a score that identifies likely errors may be useful for prioritizing review even when it does not change the underlying classifier's predictions. Selective classification formalizes a related objective by examining the trade-off between the fraction of predictions retained and the error rate among those predictions (Geifman and El-Yaniv, 2017).

This study investigates whether disagreement between EfficientNet-B0 and EfficientNet-B4 provides useful information for detecting incorrect predictions beyond confidence alone. Three signals are compared for each target model: confidence-based uncertainty, a binary indicator of disagreement between the models, and a weighted combination of the two. Combination weights are selected using validation data and evaluated on a held-out test split. Performance is assessed using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC), the area under the precision-recall curve (AUPRC), and precision, recall, and F1 score when flagging the highest-risk 20% of test predictions. Paired bootstrap comparisons are used to characterize uncertainty in differences between methods.

The study makes three empirical contributions. First, it compares confidence-only, disagreement-only, and combined error-detection signals for two target models under a shared filename-deduplicated test split. Second, it examines whether the contribution of disagreement is consistent across the two models rather than assuming that it provides a uniform benefit. Third, it reports both ranking performance and fixed-budget flagging metrics, alongside paired uncertainty estimates and explicit limitations concerning patient- or lesion-level independence.

The results should be interpreted as an evaluation of error detection within the available dataset and split. Filename-level deduplication does not establish patient- or lesion-level independence, and the study does not establish clinical utility or external validity.
